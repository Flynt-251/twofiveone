Usual Computer Science modules are very straightforward: answers are usually a matter of yes and no. **For this module, that couldn't be further from the truth.** Here, we'll be asking questions like, *Is it right to own a mobile phone*, *Should we use robots to reduce human labour*, and *Should the internet even exist*? There is no clear-cut yes or no answer to these questions, so instead we debate different points of view. Namely, we apply different *ethical frameworks* to shape our arguments. At the very least it's better than just using our own opinions, which are (whether you like it or not) subject to bias.

Ethics help to address what is "right" and what is "wrong", and in turn, guide moral decisions and how to live a "good life". Again, this is all very subjective, and what one person thinks is "right" may be downright shocking to you! But we should make a reasonable attempt to listen anyway. Ethics largely branch off into three types:

- **Meta Ethics** - In order to bake a cake, you must invent the universe. In other words, this seeks to define what exactly good and bad is, before we can define what good or bad behaviours are. We don't cover this in this module.
- **Normative Ethics** - Tries to find methods by which we can define what is the morally correct action. That being said, this is often wrought with conflicting ideas.
- **Applied Ethics** - Using moral theories to make a decision about a current, real issue. For example, using moral theories to decide if it is okay to distribute art without consent from its artist.

An **ethical framework** is something that provides methods by which we can resolve an *ethical dilemma*, where rights and wrongs can conflict. Think about shoplifting for example. Is it wrong to steal a loaf of bread if you can't afford it? Your gut instinct may say *yes, because it's illegal*, which is very fair, but read back over that question. The alternative is, you don't, so you starve. So what's more important then - following the law and what is deemed socially correct, or your own survival? Reasonable people disagree, so this is what ethical frameworks try to help with. However, some frameworks may reach the same conclusion about a problem, and even people applying the same framework may reach differing conclusions.

Many normative frameworks are based on **consequentialism**, which, to put it simply, means *"the end justifies its means"*, so an action is justifiable, if it leads to a good outcome. With stealing a loaf of bread, the consequence is you are able to feed yourself for a day and survive a little longer. There is also a small loss on the part of the shop you steal from, which some will use to condemn this action. It is then up to the framework on which consequence deserves priority.

Let's start with three normative frameworks: Utilitarianism, Virtue Ethics, and Deontology.

## Happymaxxing - Utilitarianism

*The greatest good for the greatest number*

Utilitarianism is focused on whatever actions maximise happiness and well-being the most. The "Utility" of this is producing benefits, so the best action is whatever has the best overall consequences.

For instance, utilitarian approaches may justify data collection in social media, as it can promote better well-being for its users, such as using this data to promote useful resources, namely mental health support if someone's usage shows that they could benefit from them. How much that actually happens in practice is anybody's guess.

This immediately raises an important question though, and that is, *how do you quantify utility*? How do you measure short-term and long-term consequences in relation to each other? Given that utilitarianism itself is a set of frameworks itself, the answer to this is not well-defined. For instance, let's consider piracy: this enables the enjoyment of entertainment, art and games for thousands of people, who may not otherwise be able to access such things, so this leads to greater happiness for many, many people. But, what about the artist, the development team, the studio, that made that work? Is their loss justified by the pleasure gained by those who pirated it?

Then there are some additional problems: *what even is "good" in the first place?* Do you (the reader) and I (the writer) hold the same definitions of this? Is it even right to say that the consequences alone are enough to justify an action? Some applied frameworks quantify specific kinds of good, like well-being, preferences and minimisation of harm. One such example is the [WHOQOL](https://www.who.int/tools/whoqol), which is an assessment of physical and mental health, independence, social relationships, personal beliefs and the environment, used in medical environments.

We said that utilitarianism itself encompasses a set of frameworks, so let's go over three big ones:

- **Act Utilitarianism** - Evaluate each individual action based on how much good/harm it causes. So stealing a loaf of bread is justifiable as the ability to live another day outweighs the negligible loss the shop makes.
- **Rule Utilitarianism** - Consider the long-term, what rules, when followed by all, would result in the greatest good overall? So if people all start stealing bread, this causes serious harm to businesses, so why should it be justifiable for just one person to do it?
- **Preference Utilitarianism** - The best action is what is most preferable among everyone affected by it. You'd like to be able to feed yourself for the day, and realistically, while the shop loses money, the cost is small enough for it to be shrugged off.

Much of utilitarianism relies on prediction, which is at best unreliable, and it doesn't value individual people's rights, like with the piracy example we covered before. Furthermore, what benefits a majority may seriously harm a minority - why should this minority not be included in the first place?

## Inside you are two wolves - Virtue Ethics

*Don't put this out of context, please.*

Virtue ethics revolves around building a certain character, rather than evaluating actions. It recognises that to be ethical is a lifelong process, and that we should aim to collect certain virtues so that in the future, we are well-equipped enough to make reasonable decisions using *practical wisdom*.

A *virtue* is some characteristic that enables somebody to live well, flourish and contribute to society. Consider the phrase "\_\_\_\_ is a virtue" - what words come to mind? Patience? Moderation? Think about how these encourage well-being. Of course, these don't have to be abstract, one-word concepts, but also things like *environmental awareness*, or *drive for learning*.

So would it be virtuous to steal a loaf of bread? Most likely not - doing so provides one with immediate gratification at the expense of the shop, some may call it greed, which is certainly not a virtuous characteristic. Note that I said *probably* there, as a virtue ethicist could also argue that self-preservation is a virtue - the ability to keep yourself alive ensures your own well-being, even if that comes out the cost of digging out of someone else's pocket. Told you this wasn't exactly straightforward!

Virtue Ethics goes in the opposite direction of Utilitarianism: it focuses on personal development more than actions, fallibly so. And as we saw above, many virtues will conflict with each other, making discussions complicated and subject to personal bias.

In the context of computing, we may try and develop applications which prioritise the upholding of certain virtues, like constructive conversations on a micro-blogging platform (i.e. twitter, but actually palatable). This contrasts typical metrics-based approaches, where clicks and cash are the priority (but that's a conversation for another post not so far away...).

## It is your duty to stop being late to lectures - Deontology

*Insert WikiHow person pointing at mirror image*

Deontology is about assessing the morality of actions based on rules, or duties. Duty for duty's sake. The consequences of said action are secondary, so even if an action has a good outcome, if the rules surrounding it call it bad, then it is a bad action first and foremost. This would mean stealing bread would be out of the question - the overwhelming majority of people agree stealing is wrong, AND it's illegal. End of story. As Kant puts it, ethics is about doing your duty, not chasing good outcomes, i.e., following the rules and acting as you should.

By defining a set of rules to begin with, this creates a far more consistent and universalisable moral framework, where we encourage critical thinking, and promote people to act responsibly and, well, ethically.

But is it realistic to assume it is possible to create a universal set of rules? The unfortunate thing about the modern world is almost everything has nuance to it, and what one person may think is a perfectly reasonable rule, another may completely disagree with. And we completely ignore the consequences - is it really that much better that we risk our death just because it's generally considered wrong to steal? In the modern day, we usually discern between deeper and lesser duties. i.e. some duties overrule others - in our example, self-preservation overrules not stealing. And where we still have conflicts, we should use moral reasoning to reach a conclusion - intent is an important variable in these cases.

Now we move to applied frameworks - these are a little more rigid.

## The steamed hams guy - Principlism

*Wait no, that's Principle Skinner.*

Principlism originates in biomedical ethics, and guides decision making using four key moral principles:

- **Autonomy** - Does this respect an individual's right to their own choice and control over their own life?
- **Beneficence** - Does this promote the welfare/well-being of others?
- **Non-maleficence** - Does this minimise/prevent harm?
- **Justice** - Is this fair, i.e. distribute benefits and burden equally?

This is, indeed, far less flexible than anything we've seen before - principlism gives us common ground to work with, rather than leaving these up to the ethicist. While not as forgiving as the others, this is a good way to break up those pesky conflicting values we saw earlier, particularly in professional contexts.

With principlism, we may argue against piracy, as this doesn't respect an artist/creator's rights to distribute their own work as they want to, nor does it minimise the losses that they would make. Furthermore, it certainly isn't fair on them. Of course, you may argue for or against this judgement within this space - does the creator affect your position on the matter, e.g. what if the creator actively harms a minority of people?

## That's a great framework! - AI4People

*Don't yes-man me.*

AI4People builds upon Principlism and adds on *Explicability*, and, of course, puts them in the context of AI (not just Generative AI, I might add).

- **Autonomy** - AI should respect an individual's right to their own choice and control over their own life.
- **Beneficence** - AI should promote human welfare/well-being.
- **Non-maleficence** - AI should minimise/prevent harm. This includes preventing bias, discrimination, privacy violation, etc.
- **Justice** - Are the benefits/pitfalls of AI distributed fairly? Again, we should be preventing bias.
- **Explicability** - People should be able to understand why and how AI systems reach a particular conclusion, and know who is responsible for it.

That last point is in a bit of trouble considering nobody knows how the hell Google AI decided that putting glue on a pizza was a good idea.

## Paternal Issues - PAPA

*Okay aren't we getting a little personal here haha*

PAPA is concerned with (again), four ethical issues related to information technology.

- **Privacy** - Who has access to whose personal information, with what conditions?
- **Accuracy** - Who is responsible for ensuring data is correct, and what do we do if it's wrong?
- **Property** - Who has ownership over this data/information/software, and how is this enforced?
- **Accessibility** - Who should have access to this data, and at what cost? Should *everyone* have it, and how would they get access?

Our little piracy example is very relevant here. The content should only be shared under the creator's discretion, and it is their property, so they are responsible for controlling how it is shared. So, piracy is a direct violation of all the principles of PAPA.

While it is good practice to work from a set of principles, and is certainly more practical than some vague idea of "good and bad", this way of thinking still doesn't account for the wider picture in some cases. While we may intend for a particular technology to be used in a certain way, we typically aren't accounting for every scenario. Let's see a couple of applied frameworks that let us do this.

## As always, people ruin everything - Socio-Technical Theory

*Just saying, no humans, no problems! Until we start talking about existentialism.*

Socio-technical theory recognises that technical systems and social systems work together to shape each other - we use technology in a certain way, that shapes part of our culture, and this influences how that system then evolves. Just look at texting for instance: character limits meant people began acronymise certain phrases, like "Laugh out loud" into LOL, "On my way" to OMW or "Not gonna lie, this shit pisses me off" to NGLTSPMO (okay yes I'm taking the piss with that one). This was reflected into later mobile technology, with smartphones auto-filling these terms, and in turn, much of today's society has evolved to adopt smartphone technology. We're intertwined!

In practice, this is used in workplaces to identify design goals, and by people to promote autonomy and adaptability. This is largely applied to technical subsystems, which may include algorithms or technical infrastructure, then social subsystems may refer to organisations, the law, shared moral values, or company policy/culture.

Let's think of (oh boy, here we go again) the Therac-25, and why it ultimately became the ethical software engineering nightmare it is infamous for today. Of course, the software itself was developed without careful consideration, merely porting over old code without properly testing, or with much consideration for how the new machine worked compared to older models. The developer(s?) likely never gave this much thought, and cared more about *getting a job done*, than considering how important it would be to slow down and remove the flaws.

Then, there's the operators whose priority was, *get patients in, blast them with radiation, get them out*. Radiotherapy sessions were now quick to carry out and, if you're doing this multiple times a day, are a repetitive task that you can quickly bang out, just like how you navigate your phone: you know all the entries to fill out, you now all the keyboard shortcuts, and so on, so you motor through everything. This is part of the notable *Malfunction 54* - which isn't exactly an error per se, but rather an unreliable way to delay administration of radiation so that the machine could adjust itself properly. The problem is, you can override a lot of these errors with `P`. So, if you're used to seeing these messages, you'll just gloss over them without really thinking about it... until you hear the patient screaming in agony.

It's easy to pin the blame for these sorts of incidents on one entity, but these are usually the result of a myriad of faults. It also doesn't help that within any social setting, *there will be conflicts*, so even the system itself may contain multiple conflicts in values - think about how social media sites signpost to mental health support services, but will often display troubling content for the sake of engagement. It's not a case of "who should win", but rather, "what's the best compromise", so we should use ethical metrics and, really, just have good accountability and transparency. This leads us into our last section...

## What do YOU have inside? - Value Sensitive Design (VSD)

*Insert catchy jingle from well-known hardware manufacturer*

VSD is all about incorporating human values into technology design. No design can truly be value-neutral, so it's an issue we should address *before* we even start building anything. This consists of three core methods:

- **Conceptual Investigations** - Who are the stakeholders, and what are the values, that are at risk?
- **Empirical Investigations** - Theorising is good, but here we get some real-world insight from users.
- **Technical Investigations** - Implementing the design decisions, with the values we've set out to incorporate.

Let's go back to our deadly radiation machines. Patients, doctors, nurses, software engineers and machine manufacturers are all notable stakeholders, and values such as welfare, good physical and mental health and safety are all at risk here. As such, we would want to conduct interviews, workshops and/or questionnaires with all of these groups, to get their understanding of radiotherapy, and see their perspective. We then use this information to build a system that puts safety first, keeping in mind how each stakeholder's values should shape features of the system. For instance, keeping in mind that operators of the machine want the system to be as straightforward and simple to use as possible, we want to make sure we narrow the margin for error as much as we reasonably can.

Further to this, **Privacy by design** is an example of VSD where we try to respect an individual's right to privacy and data protection as much as we can. We anticipate where a user's privacy may be at risk, keep privacy the default setting, and maintain said privacy throughout the data's lifecycle.
