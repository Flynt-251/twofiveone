Most if not all of us will be going into some sort of professional work, and so the way we conduct ourselves in these environments is a major issue that should be discussed, especially in respect to many of the issues we face in the computing industry - is our perception on issues such as data privacy, AI or commercialisation suitable for a professional environment, where we can appropriately write good code and document our decisions clearly? We should also prioritise values such as integrity, confidentiality, inclusion and accountability.

As such, *Professional Ethics* explores how we should act, in a professional setting, which lies in a gulf between what is legal, and what is right. We can very easily argue that something which is illegal is morally right, such as committing manslaughter for the sake of self-defence, but we cannot necessarily take such liberties in a professional environment. This may even go beyond legal policy, if we're discussing issues for which no legislation exists, often referred to as *policy vacuums* - AI is a big talking point here, as we continue to wait for regulations to be made.

## Stop! You've violated the Law!

*Wait... laws on this sorcery are yet to exist. Carry on, traveller.*

The trouble with making regulations is, everything moves way too f\*\*\*ing fast by comparison, and dealing with one particular system may involve multiple different pieces of policy to be applied, some of which may conflict with each other. The four main issues identified by the UK Government are:

- **Fragmentation** - There are more than ten different regulators for technology, whose mandates can overlap and even cause contradictions.
- **Pacing** - The tech industry greatly outpaces the rate at which regulatory systems can respond to it, though acting too early itself can be a risk. The time at which we should regulate is its own balancing act.
- **Skills** - The CAA, ICO and others have struggled to obtain appropriately skilled individuals.
- **Incentives** - Writing regulations is about making trade-offs (since you're deciding what values to prioritise), and the rewards/outcomes for this are not clear compared to say, working for those developing the technology in the first place.

> Sourced from [The Pro-innovation Regulation of Technologies Review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/64118f0f8fa8f555779ab001/Pro-innovation_Regulation_of_Technologies_Review_-_Digital_Technologies_report.pdf) published by HM Government

To try and streamline the regulatory processes for all these new-fangled technologies, the [Regulatory Innovation Office](https://www.techuk.org/resource/what-is-the-regulatory-innovation-office-and-what-is-it-nots.html) was set up in October 2024 to "identify and address significant regulatory barriers holding back science and technology development". It is focused on Engineering Biology, Space and Space Launch, AI and digital in healthcare and Connected and Autonomous Technology.

[Clark et al (2011)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160791X1100042X) 🔒 state that while these vacuums do exist, we can get at least some guidance on what the acceptable practices are, by evaluating both the existing current laws, and the unexpressed societal norms surrounding it. They suggest a list of guidelines to use, namely evaluating both from expressed and unexpressed norms what is right and wrong, separating what is ethical and what is lawful, involve people from the impacted culture and recognise that even if there is no disagreement over something, that does not make an action "good".

## You've heard of DCS, now get ready for BCS

*But then where's ACS and CCS?*

The **British Computer Society** is a professional body that represents people working in the IT industry and as such, provides a charter of what its purpose is:

> "The objects for which the Society is hereby constituted shall be to promote the study and practice of Computing and to advance knowledge and education therein for the benefit of the public" [BCS Royal Charter](https://www.bcs.org/media/1447/royal-charter.pdf)

That charter sets out a number of things that BCS does to ensure this, including setting a code of conduct, promoting education in the field, setting standards of said education, establishing mechanisms to share such knowledge, promote good standards, and give advice to both the government and regulatory bodies about relevant matters.

The aforementioned code of conduct establishes four key duties for its members:

- **Public Interest** - Work in the interest of wider society, ensuring no harm to people or the environment.
- **Professional Competence and Integrity** - Only work to your own level of competence, enhancing your own knowledge where relevant and respecting other members' level of competence.
- **Duty to Relevant Authority** - Perform your professional responsibilities with respect to your authority, while being vigilant about conflicts of interest, respecting confidentiality and not exploiting someone's lack of knowledge.
- **Duty to the Profession** - You are a professional, and as such, people will look up to you. Be open-minded to improve your own standards, uphold BCS's reputation, and encourage and support others in following the correct conduct.

You should have a read through the [BCS Code of Conduct](https://www.bcs.org/media/2211/bcs-code-of-conduct.pdf) for yourself. It's not very long!

The Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) is kind of like the USA version of the BCS. It is "the world’s largest computing society". They clearly haven't seen the projections for the University of Warwick Computing Society's exec team's size yet, set to exceed the human population by 2030.

Anyway, the ACM has its own *Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct*, which is bigger than the BCS code of conduct, but instead of being a rulebook, it is a set of guidelines for ethical decision-making. It's made up of four sections:

- **General Ethical Principles** - Act in the interest of the public, avoid harm, be honest, be fair, respect the work needed to create new things, respect privacy and honour confidentiality.
- **Professional Responsibilities** - In professional work, aim to achieve high quality, maintain high standards of competence, respect the rules, give and take professional review, give useful evaluations, don't work outside your realm of competence, foster public awareness, only access resources if allowed or for the good of the public, and prioritise robustness and security.
- **Professional Leadership Principles** - Ensure public good remains priority, properly maintain the social responsibilities of your organisation, manage resources to improve quality of life, pursue policies that reflect the priorities of this code, give members useful professional opportunities, take care when modifying/retiring systems, and especially be careful with systems that are integrated into the infrastructure of society.
- **Compliance with the Code** - Uphold, respect and promote the principles given in the code, and take action where these are not met.

For those of you who want to get back into the habit of reading before bed, but don't want to commit to a major story, here's the full [ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct](https://www.acm.org/binaries/content/assets/about/acm-code-of-ethics-and-professional-conduct.pdf).

## And a couple more things to consider...

[**ISO 26000**](https://www.iso.org/iso-26000-social-responsibility.html) is not a usual management standard, and so it cannot be certified. Instead, it gives a framework to ensure organisations and their members hold a common understanding of social responsibility, and gives guidance on how to make decisions around this value. Namely, its principles are accountability, transparency, ethical behaviour, respect for stakeholder interests, respect for the law, respect for international norms, and respect for human rights.

**Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG)** is a principle that prioritises social and environmental issues, as well as corporate governance, a term that was first brought up in 2004 by financial institutions alongside the United Nations. Environmentally, can cover an organisation's energy usage, their waste handling, and conservation of natural resources. As for the social side of things, this refers to interactions involving the organisation itself - are people's health and safety prioritised for instance? Does the organisation provide a positive impact to their local community? Investors use ESG to determine if a company has a positive impact, which may attract them into investing in that company.

With these standards, and the frameworks we covered previously, we have a good foundation with which we can start discussing the big problems in tech.
