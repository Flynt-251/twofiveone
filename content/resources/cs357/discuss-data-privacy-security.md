## Nobody cared who I was until I put on the mask

*And now they wanna take pictures with me and boop my snoot. Ahem.*

**Anonymity** on the internet is obviously a big discussion point here, and a concept that you yourself might be familiar with. Or at least, a close approximation to it: **Pseudonymity**, which describes one's use of a pseudonym to identify themselves, but not quite full anonymity. It's like wearing a ski mask - yes it covers your face, but anyone can see if you're there, and if they know you well, they know who you are. We use **pseudonyms** all the time, think of your gamertag on your Xbox or Steam account for instance. You might even assign yourself multiple pseudonyms to split up your digital life and make it more difficult for people to trace every aspect of your life back to you.

Pseudonyms are not the only means to hide your identity, however. There are also things like VPNs, email aliases, credit card aliases, and anti-tracking web browsers which you can also use to make you harder to identify. There are also plenty of forums which anonymise their users. At first, it's easy to see all this as an inherently good thing - if you're a journalist or whistleblower, then those who may wish to strike you down will have a harder time finding who you are. In the same vein, you can also hide aspects of your identity which might be used to discriminate you.

However, when your actions can't be traced back to you... you become relieved of any consequences, so unfortunately, because humans suck, anonymity is highly desirable for those wishing to act undesirably. Think stalkers, criminal organisations, political extremists or foreign entities wishing to spread misinformation or propaganda - being untraceable is perfect for letting such people thrive.

So, is it worth letting criminals and genuinely horrible people roam free of their consequences, or would it be better if, even if requiring lots of tools, everyone could be tied back to their real identity? Again, remember that there's no one right answer to this question, and even if there were, the answer lies somewhere in the middle. On one hand, most people reasonably agree that people should be able to be held accountable for something awful they did, but at the same time, if we give authority figures the ability to identify anyone they can... they in turn are liable to abuse this power.

Let's consider something like the Online Safety Act. This now requires age verification for any internet site that contains adult content, things like violent movies, porn, or gambling. On paper, this is a great idea, as this ensures that only adults can access those things. However, when you start putting this idea into context, things start to fall apart. Discord has implemented age verification into its platform, however it ended up [suffering a data breach](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8jmzd972leo) with the company that provided this service. [This resulted in some government ID images getting leaked](https://discord.com/press-releases/update-on-security-incident-involving-third-party-customer-service), which is a huge problem for those affected - they're now at a much higher risk of identity theft. This raises the question then, if an adult wants to access adult content, should they be required to upload their government ID or provide sensitive data? I'd, of course, argue not. We could get into the conversation about how parents need to get off their lazy asses and actually raise their kids, but that's getting off-topic.

## A little more on data collection and breaches

Reject Convenience has a really good video which talks about the [different types of data brokers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iX3JT6q3AxA) there are, which give some insight about how data is collected and used. There are many types of companies who collect different sorts of data, but none of them are immune to data breaches. Heck, even a company like Equifax, whose data collection you can't opt out of, [is not immune to data breaches](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/business-41192163).

Even then, you might not even need to hack a company to get hold of millions of records of personal data and contact details. [Dark Web users were able to scrape millions of records from LinkedIn](https://cybernews.com/news/stolen-data-of-500-million-linkedin-users-being-sold-online-2-million-leaked-as-proof-2/), for example. This wasn't the result of a hack, it was simply aggregated from profiles that were available publically - it makes you wonder why people would give away such information so carelessly, but it's important to remember that privacy has been devalued greatly over the years. *"Who cares? They have my data anyway!"* Except things quickly go south once it's more than just the collectors who have that data. There are some focal points to consider when discussing the impacts of data breaches:

- **Personal Identity** - Your identity is your own, and for it to be your own, you need some level of privacy. When that privacy is violated, that's your identity at risk - there's a reason it's called identity theft!
- **Autonomy** - People have more autonomy when less information is known about them, and autonomy is an important part of the human experience (especially in my opinion!). Think about it - if fewer people know who you are, you have greater autonomy in starting your own relationships. Speaking of...
- **Social Relationships** - Information is a big portion of what make social relationships so special - there may be that secret only your closest friends know about, a group of you who share very intimate thoughts about yourself and your identity, or maybe a company where you are all held together by the skills and information you share there. If such information gets into the wild, what then?

Of course, you can't have a data breach if the data was never handed out in the first place! The other important aspect to consider is what data is collected, and how badly it violates people's right to privacy, especially depending on whether the subjects are aware of it. Nowadays, we willingly give out our names, email addresses, phone numbers and so on without a second thought - we might not even realise we're doing this sometimes! After all, when was the last time you read a privacy policy? Are those privacy policies even good, or adhered to? Are companies even collecting data that you don't explicitly hand over? This brings us nicely over to the types of privacy violations.

- **Intrusion** - Entering forcefully into something you do not have consent to access, to steal information.
- **Information Misuse** - Taking legitimate data, and using it for something not originally intended. Think of scandals like Cambridge Analytica, or more broadly, the act of taking your data and selling it to other companies.
- **Interception of Information** - Eavesdropping, wiretapping, packet sniffing, whatever you want to call it.
- **Information Matching** - The process of cross-referencing multiple datasets do make conclusions outside the intention for each of them.
	- Oddly, this is exactly how advertising works, despite being anonymous. You can uniquely identify an individual by using several boolean data points. This can also be used to make predictions, [such as figuring out if someone's pregnant via their shopping habits](https://www.forbes.com/sites/kashmirhill/2012/02/16/how-target-figured-out-a-teen-girl-was-pregnant-before-her-father-did/).

## Is there any hope for us all??

We simply have to wait for regulations to catch up. Wait a minute, that's a bad idea. It's better to be loud and annoying to get lawmakers to push for better privacy laws, though that's starting to veer in another territory. On the other hand, it's easy to admit defeat and embrace the information age, as we have all of our data pulled from us uncontrollably.

My view is things will get worse before they get better, but there are steps you can take to ensure your own privacy. Heck, here are some things you could do RIGHT NOW:

- Change your browser settings to delete cookies when you close the window. Note that this means you'll need to log back into some sites, though you can set up a whitelist.
- Use ad blockers. Look, even the FBI recommends you use one.
- Reduce your use of social media: easier said than done for many of us, but that's where a lot of data points are collected.
- When browsing the internet, try entering URLs directly instead of searching. This bypasses collections on search engines. You might consider using a Bangs extension such as [Yangs](https://github.com/dmlls/yang).

Again, I'll also point to [Reject Convenience](https://www.youtube.com/@rejectconvenience), who has videos explaining VPNs, cookies and data deletion services. They've also got a good video called "What if you can't reject convenience?" which gives some ideas to help you consider what sorts of data collection are permissible for you, and what you're willing to give up. Their videos are about helping you learn and grow, not fearmongering, which is a lovely change of pace.

On the subject of browsers, [Google got in trouble over Chrome's Incognito Mode](https://www.wired.com/story/google-chrome-incognito-mode-data-deletion-settlement/) [(Archived Copy 🔓)](https://archive.is/ytr45) a while ago, claiming they were deleting billions of records as a result. Nothing was wrong with incognito mode really, but the way it was presented was misleading, touting it as "private browsing". I mean, come on, it's Google Chrome, dipshit. The splash page when you hit Ctrl+Shift+N does warn that even though history and cookies aren't saved, this naturally does nothing to stop tracking from third parties. This isn't to say private browsing is useless, I personally like being able to look at something without altering my history and hence fuck up my autofill, that uh... is a lesson I learned the hard way.

## Now it's your turn

If you're reading this, then at least some of you are somewhat interested in doing research and gathering some data then. Even if not that, you may be doing this as a software engineer. That raises a question then - how do we set a good example? Well, there's questions we need to ask, and this happens at every stage of the dataset's life.

- Gathering Data
	- Did we have permission to get it?
	- Is there personally identifiable information (PII) involved?
	- Are the subjects aware of this?
	- Did this data get gathered fairly?
	- Could there be bias in this data?
- Storing Data
	- Can we ensure controlled, secure access?
	- Is the data anonymised/is there a good reason it isn't?
	- Are backups made, and are people aware of them?
	- Can we guarantee integrity of the data?
- Using Data - What's the purpose?
	- For profit? Targeting? Informing policy?
	- Could this cause harm to someone?
	- Is this being used fairly?
	- Have you made this purpose clear?
- Sharing Data
	- Do the subjects know this will happen and with whom?
	- Is this shared data accurate?
	- Is the data replicable?
	- Is the data anonymised/traceable?
	- Who should be given access, and could they misuse this data?

Datasets are incredibly important and valuable. We go over bias in another discussion, but dataset bias can have very real and devastating impacts, such as [reinforcing institutional racism](https://www.amnesty.org.uk/knowledge-hub/all-resources/automated-racism-report/). It also matters a lot what we do with said data: is it ethical to use data for predictive policing? Or will this lead to an INGSOC-like policing system where the wrong thoughts results in criminal arrests?

The bottom line is, we need to follow the [GDPR](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/contents). The basics of it is, ensure you collect data for its intended and justifiable purpose, store it securely with the subject's consent, and respect their decision when they ask for said data to be deleted. The [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/) provides additional support on how to approach use of data, and what to about your data. Lastly, there's the [Data and AI Ethics Framework](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/data-ethics-framework/data-and-ai-ethics-framework), which you should follow, and whose core values are transparency, accountability and fairness.

For my thoughts on this, just a bit of honesty and transparency can speak wonders. Don't be a dick, and respect the fact it's not your data, people are allowed to divulge what they want about themselves.
