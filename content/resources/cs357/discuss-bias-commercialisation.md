## DEVELOPERS, DEVELOPERS, DEVELOPERS!

*What are some of these CEOs smoking?*

How often have you worked on a programming project, assignment, or even just a script, and had that feeling of *"fuck this shit"* come up? Well, you're hardly alone, and we're fortunate enough in this space where the only consequence of giving up is our own personal failure. We don't lose some company money or aggravate any stakeholders.

However, for software engineers, it's a different story. An example I love to use of this is being *on call*, where essentially, you are required to forego sleep and basic human necessities to fix problems in software. It's common practice, notably with Amazon, who requires employees to install an app to their phone which sounds an alarm when their help is needed, at any time of day. Don't know about you, but that would absolutely cause me to have chronic insomnia.

It's even worse for game developers, who are often forced to work long hours and undergo "crunches" where, similarly, they forego their necessities in order to finish work on a game. Red Dead Redemption 2 developers noted they'd sometimes drink on the job to cope, Fortnite developers have complained about a culture of fear and SEVENTY hour work weeks (akin to an 8-6 job, with no rest days), and the list goes on. Is this level of suffering, and possibly even risk to health worth having buggy or unfinished software?

## Time to reject convenience

*Hey Anthony, love your vids!*

It's no secret that companies are doing everything they can to squeeze as much money as they can out of online platforms and spaces. Just look at the online advertising industry, rife with tracking, cookies, dark patterns and fingerprinting. There's a lot of useful data to be extracted on the internet (something to be discussed another time), often without most people realising it.

What's more important in this section is **Dark Patterns**, or designs in software to influence user decisions. Think things like checkboxes where you *really* need to check what they say, buttons being greyed out despite still being clickable, or making the "INSTALL NOW" button way bigger than the "No thanks, I hate fun" button. You see what I'm trying to get at. But this can get way, WAY worse, such as Adobe's infamous cancellation fees which force users to pay exorbitant amounts of money to end their subscription, instead of letting it roll over for yet another year.

What scares me the most though, is user engagement. This is talking about the recommendation systems on platforms such as TikTok or Instagram which are designed, solely, to keep you on the platform? Why? Because it makes them money of course! But, what's engaging isn't always what's fun or joyful - think about it, after a while you'd get bored and go do something else. Instead, these algorithms play with your emotions, showing you controversial content, things that might make you uncomfortable, or even... cause your own suicide? There are multiple cases of people entering self-harm forums or being shown such content and being thrown down a very, very dark path.

## Let's go gambling!

*If you want to gamble, for the love of god play Balatro, Raccoin, Cloverpit or some other roguelike.*

What's a very profitable online industry? Gambling! Online Gambling sites are full of dark patterns, and they're not hard to see at all, promises of free spins, additional credits, money back on losses and so on, which are all meant to replace the dark patterns of real casinos, which don't have windows or clocks to ensure you spend as much time there as possible. These online platforms may do even shadier stuff like give free credits to lure someone back into the platform if they don't play for a while.

But what about gambling, where you don't win money? What about... weapon skins, or virtual items or... a buff cyborg anime wolf?? Wait- \*cough\* that was way too specific... What I'm getting at now is, of course, **Lootboxes and Gacha Games**. While you pay money for certain in-game items, these don't promise you any potential money back in return, rather you gain items which are scored by "rarity", with rarer items being more powerful, sought after or, really, just hyped up. These really encourage that "one more try" mindset, which is why so many people call these things gambling. And even worse, they target teenage kids! Whether you deem this gambling is up to you, though this is a question currently being debated in law.

**Roblox** games are particularly notorious for this, not just Genshin Impact, Zenless Zone Zero or Fortnite. If you don't know, Roblox is a platform where people can make their own games, or, \*ahem\* "experiences", and can charge "Robux", or in-game currency, for items in the game. This could be an exclusive perk, special items or... you guessed it, lootboxes. A particular example of this is Pet Simulator X, which allows players to buy "eggs" with Robux to possibly unlock rare pets. The actual gameplay? Lacklustre at best. But the addictiveness of eggs is what drives engagement and profit for the developers, keeping it at the top.

## Racist Conditions

*Not to be confused with race conditions*

**Algorithmic Bias** is where an algorithm, intentionally or not, disproportionally favours or disadvantages one group over another. Think things like, an AI agent working in the process of hiring at a software, who favours male white or Asian candidates, for example. On that actually, Amazon did get in trouble back in 2018 because in its hiring process, [it unfairly favoured men over women](https://www.reuters.com/article/idUSKCN1MK0AG/). We like to think that this sort of thing is impossible, but really, it's not. Especially in the case of AI, where datasets can be unfairly trained more on white individuals over black. Or what about failing to understand the real world? Let's bring back a classic, fictional anecdote from CS261. Sorry for the scare.

> A group of engineers design a robot bartender. They account for various different scenarios in which the robot may be asked to make drinks. They account for a customer asking the robot to make one drink, two drinks, three... infinite drinks, zero drinks, minus one drink, half a drink... you get the idea. After all of that testing, the team finally trials the robot in an actual bar.
> 
> The first customer walks into the bar and asks the simple question: "Where's the bathroom?"
> 
> The robot explodes.

You of course know the moral behind this story - you need to consider everything when you're designing something that the public is going to interact with. So have fast food companies done this when installing AI drive-thru agents? Uhh... not really. It's caused stupid TikToks like [this one](https://www.tiktok.com/@typical_redhead_/video/7192248491853303086), where someone had over $200 of McNuggets added to their order. I mean, if I'm drunk I'll happily have some, but they can't be THAT good.

Now for a bit more of a darker example, self-driving cars, while trying to avoid talking about the trolley problem so much. ### What causes bias?

Bias in computing is a multi-faceted issue, underpinned by many factors. What we should mainly consider is...

- Human Bias - through groupthink, cognitive bias, and so on.
- Systemic Bias - whether this be historical, societal, or even institutional.
- Statistical Bias - Poor sampling, incorrect processing, or inappropriate usage.

There's a lovely paper written by NIST that you can read [here](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.1270.pdf).

Bias in this context is obviously harmful, but we can in turn, use bias to undo bias, this can include augmenting datasets, altering training parameters, and so on. I almost like to think of it as akin to the bias values used in neural networks.
