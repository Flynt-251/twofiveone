Remember when the British Empire was a thing? No? Ah right, yeah, because that's a very old thing at this point. But during the 19th century, **Colonisation**, where one group controls another's land and people, using the resources as though it is their own domain, was rampant, especially with us Brits. We're still kind of infamous for claiming that things are ours, when they're certainly not. Curries are not a British invention, even saying chicken tikka masala is a British dish is contentious statement in my opinion. But, the impact of colonisation, **Colonialism** has had a devastating long-term impact, where we still feel the effects of otherism, sexism, racism, heteronormativity and xenophobia that were brought on by our ancestors.

## There's many ways to slice a Pi

But what's most relevant here is the **Digital Divide** that colonialism is at least partially responsible for - even in the same country digital resources are not shared equally - while you might be looking at this article on a contemporary laptop with a text editor of your choice alongside it, someone else might be looking at it on a phone that's over five years old, and they might be closer to you than you think! They might even be working with even older hardware and software, which won't be able to keep up.

These people aren't able to access digital means of socialising easily, or might not even be able to get access to information about their local community, leading to isolation. What's more, without access to proper devices, they might have limited educational access and worse digital literacy, which also translates into worse digital autonomy.

Fixing the problem isn't easy though. Wiring internet into many locations, especially rural areas, can be difficult: *geographically*, if an area is unstable or prone to things like landslides or earthquakes, connections can be easily broken and are hence unreliable. Computers are usually *expensive* too, and this isn't even getting into the need of *educating* people on how to use the internet correctly! Even those in developing areas will be more incentivised to move away from those areas to urban areas for promise of better pay or other offerings.

Also, we unfortunately have to consider politics. In places where there is unrest or corruption, attempting to bridge the gap could do more harm than good - corrupt government can get ahold of technology and use it to fuel their own rhetoric or use it for unintended purposes, such as organising their military. The key way to solve this problem is through global collaboration and understanding - bridging the digital divide requires us to set aside our own culture and recognise that of the other side, to not undermine them and give them a much-deserved voice.

## The Computing Empire

As much as we may not want to admit it, Computer Science and Colonialism have an uncomfortably close relationship - we can see this embedded into the language, with things like master and slave devices (more of a legacy term), black or white hat hacking, or programming languages themselves, which force developers around the world to use English. Some even argue that the modern digital age itself facilitates colonialism, which is fairly plain to see once you consider nearly all major tech companies are Western, and nearly all of which take resources from other countries to grow.

Even the attempts that big tech makes to bridge the digital divide tend to be rather superficial and ineffective - it's not enough to provide low-cost computers and internet access, there also needs to be proper education, and building up of other infrastructure first - what good is a shiny new low-cost laptop and satellite internet when you're already in fear of your family dying from preventable diseases or dehydration or famine?

This is clearly visible in the [One Laptop Per Child initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Laptop_per_Child), an attempt to bring low-cost, educational computers to children in developing countries. While on paper, it was a great idea, each device cost $100, and governments were required to order in a minimum quantity of one million units. That's quite a sting for a government who may already be struggling. What's more, [the whole idea was considered to be very US-centric](https://web.archive.org/web/20170210165101/http://edition.cnn.com/2005/WORLD/africa/12/01/laptop/), with Africans pointing out the fact that they didn't have time to do things like research crops with their children, they would have to plant them! *"If you live in a mud hut, what use is that computer for your children who don't have a doctor within walking distance?"*, to quote the linked CNN article, and very aptly put.

Things have improved for these countries since OLPC, and more of Africa is online. But, just how many of them are aware of things like data collection, bots, misinformation campaigns, scamming, and so on? It opens these people up to further exploitation, just in the same way which we have seen in the "West".

Again, the importance of education must not be ignored, but not just for new internet users on how to navigate it, but for us on what their priorities are. But this is merely part of the full solution - remember that overcoming colonialism requires us to also set aside our own culture, enable global collaboration, introduce global legislation, and promote a more varied internet, that doesn't demand its users to speak English. Again, things are getting better, let's not lose sight of that - for example, Khyam Allami is a musician, who, back in the mid '00s, was studying electronic music. He discovered a problem, much of the music software was not fit for, say, Arabic music, which includes things like quarter-tones, which were never present in Western classical music, which most software is based on. His solution then, was to create Apotome, a music generator that removes this western bias, and gives the user way more tunings to select from.

## Batteries not included

There's a lot that goes into your tech: plastics, aluminium, gold, copper, silica, silicon, and that's not even talking about all that 'leccy! Where does all of this come from? Well, you're probably not gonna like the answer. The tech industry contributes a LOT to the environment, namely releasing tonnes (literally) of CO2 into the environment, and that's all from the very simple things like taking a selfie, all the way to training gigantic AI models in data centres. What's more, hardware and physical media produces SO MUCH WASTE. Think of things like smartphones - Apple and Samsung have notably created ecosystems which encourage users to upgrade on a yearly basis. Not only is this very expensive, but... where do all of those old phones go? And let's not get into [what Atari did back in 1983](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atari_video_game_burial)...

So, what's the problem with getting all 66 materials needed to make a phone?

- Firstly, we don't have infinite resources, this isn't Minecraft.
	- And we're pretty bad about recycling what we have, too.
	- What happens when we run out of each resource, who gets what?
	- Do we prioritise phones? Medical Equipment? Weaponry??
- Just like Spice in Dune, people fight for materials.
	- Many of these are from developing countries, with poorly equipped or poorly aligned governments.
	- As such, military groups may fight for control, harming local civilians and economy.
	- Notably, Brazil has seen increased disease, trafficking and drug smuggling as a result.
- And wait, is this even moral?
	- Children as young as 15 may be forced to mine these resources, leading to early graves.
	- Human rights are not the priority, it's quotas at the forefront.

It might be appropriate to ask some morbid questions as a result, such as, *how many deaths are acceptable for an iPhone?*, or, *is the promise of AI worth the global exploitation it brings in its path?* And as a result, we may make some strong arguments, from the extreme like, *we should stop using all computers!*, to *we should limit the use of streaming services, since they're the biggest portion of internet traffic*. It's important that, regardless of what we choose, we try our best not to act based on gut feeling, but take adequate sacrifice to improve things while not drastically and unsustainably changing society.
