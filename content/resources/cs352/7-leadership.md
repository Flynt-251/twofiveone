## The illusion of choice

An important part of being a leader is being **decisive**, making decisions quickly, and making them WELL. This is not the same as being impulsive, decisiveness implies the ability to consider the necessary information quickly and apply it. Additionally, it's important to be considerate about possible bias. Decisiveness is important as even if you make an erroneous decision, that can be learned on, plus self-confidence is infectious! One thing that can help with this is the **PrOACT** method.

1. Define the **Problem**. The first step to addressing any issue is to ensure we fully comprehend what it actually is.
2. Then, figure out what the solution's **objectives** should be - converging on a solution might seem like a good idea, but it's getting ahead of yourself.
3. And once you have considered a solution, keep the **alternatives** in mind. The best choice can only be as good as its next-best alterative.
4. So with these solutions worked out, consider the **consequences** of each of them: what will happen as a result of executing each of them?
5. Remember to consider **trade-offs**. This is especially important in cases where there is no one optimal solution.

With this in mind, it's relevant to know that there are different styles of leaders. These each have their own levels of decisiveness, and how much you need to improve your thought process depends on your style as a leader - are you more "on the ball" and encouraging towards your own approach, or do you let your foot off the gas and let the team manage itself?

And as for biases, here's the different kinds. There are only twenty.

- **Anchoring bias** - Being too reliant on the first piece of given information.
- **Availability heuristic** - Overestimating how important a piece of information is.
- **Bandwagon effect** - A type of groupthink.
- **Blind-spot bias** - Failing to see your own bias.
- **Choice-supportive bias** - The feeling your choice is the best.
- **Clustering illusion** - Seeing patterns that don't exist.
- **Confirmation bias** - Only listening to affirmative comments.
- **Conservatism bias** - Favouring old evidence to the new.
- **Information bias** - Seeking information when it doesn't affect action.
- **Ostrich effect** - Ignore negative information.
- **Outcome bias** - Focusing on the destination, not the journey.
- **Overconfidence**
- **Placebo** - The power of belief.
- **Pro-innovation bias** - Focusing on form over function.
- **Recency** - Prioritising the latest information over older.
- **Salience** - Focusing on the most recognisable features.
- **Selective Perception** - Expectations influencing perception.
- **Stereotyping** - Assuming things based on unrelated characteristics.
- **Survivorship bias** - Focused on surviving or positive examples.
- **Zero-risk bias** - Risk Aversion!

## There is no I in Team

*But you can't spell Team without Tea. Have a cuppa.*

The most important characteristic you can have in a team is **Trust**, so let's first see how this can cause a team to become dysfunctional...

1. In the **absence of trust**, people don't feel as though they are able to express themselves honestly...
2. And if they do, this will cause problems, so people start developing a **fear of conflict**, resulting in an "artificial harmony".
3. When we pretend to be harmonious and share consensus, no real decisions are made, in other words, there is a **lack of commitment**.
4. When nobody wants to commit to anything, **nobody wants to take accountability**.
5. So as a result, **everyone stops caring about the outcome**.

Along with trust, we also want the following characteristics in a good team:

- Respect
- Camaraderie (i.e. lightheartedness)
- Communication
- Constructive Interaction
- Values Diversity
- Optimism
- Team Leadership
- Decision-Making
- Proactivity
- Accountability
- Goals and Strategies
- Alignment

## Let me speak to your manager

*Wait, I'm the manager.*

To understand how to manage a team, you should understand how groups form and evolve. Tuckman splits the process into five stages.

1. **Forming** - The group introduces themselves to each other, and lays down some foundational ground rules. Everyone is simply still an acquaintance to each other.
2. **Storming** - Members start communicating their feelings to each other. Because strong bonds haven't formed yet, there is resistance and some minor conflict between them, and the team leadership.
3. **Norming** - Everyone begins to see themselves as part of the team, and starts to accept each other's viewpoints - they may not hold the same ones, but simply acknowledge and accept.
4. **Performing** - In the newly-found open and trusting environment, the team works together with flexibility, and the need for leadership lessens as it becomes self-sustaining.
5. **Adjourning** - The project ends, or the outcome is realised. The team may stick together, reorganise, or dissolve. This cycle repeats.

## Motivation? What's that?

*I'm really getting tired of this shit*

Generally, you can expect people to be self-motivating, however, if their basic needs (see Maslow's hierarchy) aren't met, this threatens this automatic procedure. Moreover though, we need to understand what motivates people, and what causes people to lose that motivation. These are generally a lack of support, personal conflicts, and/or overburden.

- Addressing a Lack of Support
	- Align the project to the organisation's goals
	- Ensure management is informed of everyone's status
	- Split the workload up into manageable chunks
- Dealing with Team Conflicts
	- Establish what the problem is
	- Get information from all perspectives
	- Brainstorm the possible solutions to the conflict
	- Decide the fairest solution of each of them.
- Overcoming Overburden
	- Define the scope properly
	- Manage stakeholder expectations
	- Implement a process to enable management to change
