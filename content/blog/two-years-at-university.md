A few weeks ago, I received my results for my exams. To be perfectly honest, I was worried I wouldn't quite meet my expectations this year. Sure, I felt like I could pass, but I was hoping for anything above 60% so I could move on with doing a four year course. But nevertheless, I met that goal.

So now that means I've reached the halfway point. Which feels incredibly strange given I still haven't even gotten over the fact I made it into university in the first place. Either way though, I think it's time for me to give my story and advice up to this point. But I'm not going to do a "top five tips for starting university" type of post. That just doesn't sit right with me. Instead, I'll take you through my journey and tell you what I've discovered. That way, you might be able to learn from my mistakes, and gain a real perspective on what it's like to study Computer Science at a top UK university. It's not all ups, there will be downs. It might just be one of the hardest things you put yourself through. But don't let that put you off, I'm still having the time of my life.

## Welcome to the real (small) world

Even though I was fairly local to my university, I decided to stay in halls for my first year. I figured this would allow me to get to know other people and learn to deal with them, and test my ability to survive as a single adult. Moving day alone was a bit of a chore, I was placed on the third floor of my block (with no lifts), lugging up many heavy bags and boxes, including Nahkato (who was previously named "Entrancement"). But unpacking that day was easily one of the most exciting feelings I'd ever had. I'd flicked on some music and gotten straight on with putting things away, positioning my PC setup and placing multitudes books on the shelves. I still remember that night, coming home late after a quick round of shopping, quietly eating some macaroni cheese in the shared kitchen, alone, taking in the new environment. This was my new home.

I met quite a few faces that year, across a range of subjects, including maths, engineering, literature... and from all across the world too! I wasn't exactly an outsider, nor was I super close with my flatmates: I didn't go on many outings with them, but if food was being offered for a special event, then I couldn't say no. I still remember the night when we got drunk and began walking across the whole of campus. They also gave me a cake for my birthday too, I still love you guys for that. However, I didn't get on with everyone, sometimes there were disagreements, disruptions during the night, and so on. Generally I didn't say much and just kept to myself in such cases. In retrospect I could've spoken up, but I didn't want to cause trouble.

At the same time, I gathered with some friends I met a little before uni started, and we scrambled during the first term to sort out a contract for a shared house over the next year. We managed to get a decent little place that's only a 15 minute walk away from campus, with some shops close by. Perfect for our needs, really. Fast forward a year later, and now we usually keep to our own devices and occasionally organise little meetups for food or such. Of course, even being friends, we've had our disagreements: I can't say it's very fun to get involved in these, and I admit sometimes my thoughts are clouded by my own ideals. However, at the end of the day, it's best to remember that anger clouds your thoughts, and respect is a mutual thing, give what you get.

## Hero to zero

*Okay, maybe not quite, but this bit is a little rough.*

University is when I began to be humbled. The very first example of this being a simple programming assignment: we were instructed to write some well-formatted code, and write a short prelude at the top of each file we made. I had the genius idea to also make a report alongside the assignment, and since I couldn't attach it, I instructed the marker to email me for it. Needless to say, no one ever asked for it, so I had slowed my working pace and done extra work for no reason, and ultimately it hurt my final grade for that assignment.

I also struggled with perfectionism, coming from a background where the answer was clear-cut and obvious, mainly in mathematics. However, university level mathematics isn't always about being clear-cut. Sometimes, it's about explaining things, having sound understanding, and possibly realising that your intuition isn't always right. This has been a sore point for me in anything theoretical throughout my degree, including discrete maths, formal languages or algorithms. To be honest, most of algorithms was a hot mess. and put me off doing software engineering as a career.

> Content Warning: For the rest of this section, I discuss poor mental health, depression and anxiety.

I wasn't used to failing. Throughout my time in school, I was always told I was super smart, being taken out of class for extra IQ tests, being sectioned off for advanced focus groups, or being encouraged to take further maths (I never did). I suppose then, my brains were my identity. It was only by the end of the first term of second year that I realised academia couldn't uphold this way of thinking. Coursework was piling up, concepts weren't making sense, and lectures began to feel like a waste of time. It all became a drag, I couldn't even get up at 7am every day like I used to. I remember sitting in the library, gently sobbing over my laptop, trying to work but just... sitting there, feeling sorry for myself. I felt trapped, like there was no escape, and that nobody was coming to save me.

It got to its worse point during a test at the end of term. I'd opened the paper, fairly confident. *I'm sure this will be just fine, I can get sixty easy, I'm sure*, I thought. But ten minutes in, I hit a mental block. I began to panic as my time ran out. Everything inside me told me I was going to fail. It soon became the only thing I could concentrate on. When the clock hit zero, I couldn't keep it in anymore, and I went straight into a meltdown: I lay at the desk, a hyperventilating, flushed, delirious mess. I'd failed, I was sure of it. *This wasn't me, everything had always worked out up to this point*. I *knew* what I needed to do, but I just couldn't connect the dots. That test should've been easy. It *was* easy, everyone else did fine. Nevertheless, I walked to the local pub with my friends, defeated, destroyed and disappointed in myself.

Fortunately, I later took some steps to help myself, organising an appointment with my doctor to discuss what to do, and making my university's wellbeing team aware of the situation. I also decided to do some therapy, which did ultimately help. I know this is uncomfortable to read, but what I'm trying to get across here is, yeah, it's really tough, but it's not a sign of weakness to ask for help, quite the opposite in fact! Your university has wellbeing support teams for a reason: asking for help isn't causing a burden or wasting their time, *their job is to help you*.

Additionally, where there are support systems available to you, don't dismiss them because you *think* you don't need them. I got through my A Levels without extra time or other support, so I originally dismissed such things when I started at uni. It was only after I'd hit my lowest point, that I later got additional adjustments including additional exam time and flexible coursework deadlines, which have been a massive help.

## Money, Jobs and all that boring stuff

Somewhere along the line, I had to remind myself why I was doing all of this in the first place. I think most people take a degree because they believe it's a promise of a big salary, a fancy title, or something else along those lines. Or maybe they were conditioned to think that way. I chose to do a degree because I wanted to challenge myself: did I have what it takes to perform such a big undertaking? Was learning truly my end goal? It might well be, and I think my resources page is the proof of that. Writing those notes, really, was for my own benefit rather than anyone else's. It's a manifestation of the concept of *"teach what you learned to a 10 year old"*.

And, it just so happened that it ended up helping quite a few people! I had messages telling me how useful people found the notes, asking for clarifications, or suggesting edits. It was a wonderful feeling knowing I was not only helping to teach people, but also to facilitate self-learning. It, and other things, taught me that I really get a kick out of helping people. Combine that with failed attempts at playing the internship game, and I began to realise what sort of career I wanted: I didn't want some bland corporate job, even if it meant loads of money: what use would I have with all of it in the first place? Not like I'm planning to live in London. Instead, I wanted to seek something out where the impact I was making was easy to see: IT administration in SMEs, civil service, maybe even IT services in a school. Who knows. And maybe you'll have a similar eureka moment too, when you find your "raison d'être". But there's no shame in following the majority: the reason there is a majority in the first place is because, *that route does work*.

## Belonging

In some ways, I accidentally forced myself to interact with other people by becoming a Gaming Coordinator for the computing society (UWCS). On the week I ran, I met someone else who was also running, so we both helped pack up for an event together. As it turned out, when we walked home after midnight, we realised we were both living in the same block of accommodation! We're now great friends. And of course, there were plenty more people on the exec team who I'd have four-hour conversations with, get drinks from, or send memes to. Now I was bringing myself to explore new games, new locations and new people, since as exec, it made sense for me to be at all the events UWCS offers (or at least, most of them). Before I knew it, I became fairly well-known in the society!

But, I did explore other societies too, including board games, badminton, allotment, and WUFS. Allotment was a lot of fun, actually, encouraging me to get dirty on my hands and knees, plucking weeds and fishing out all kinds of little bugs. I'd encourage giving it a try if you fancy getting off your phone. Most recently though, I've been active in WUFS, who hosts weekly board game nights on Thursdays, another way to get off your phone. I also went to my very first convention with this society, and to be honest, this experience alone helped dig me out of a hole of feeling insecure and hopeless. I wasn't interested in chasing grades anymore and seeking to be the top of my class, what was the reward realistically, when I could get just as much joy spending time in these conventions, where I could freely express myself? In that vein, student societies are the life-blood of universities, and they kinda saved my life. Give them a try.

## My final word

I'm going to end with a very cliche phrase: it's about the journey, not the destination. I think a lot of us forget that though, in a society that measures success by salaries, consumerism and status symbols. There's just no joy to be found in any of these I think. But in all of the times I have put myself forward, and stepped outside of my comfort zone, I've built life-changing stories, befriended people who care about me as a fellow human being, and gained a better understanding on how I want to lead my life. Again, I was never in this for the money, I just want to help people, to teach them new and exciting things.

I give you these stories not to put you off the idea of going to University, but to see it for what it is: it's not just a cool little piece of paper you can hang up on your wall. It's discovering who you are, what you want to do, finding friends for life, deciding what gives you motivation to keep going, and so much more. I wish someone told me that before I started. And yes, this story is a little gritty, parts of it are harsh and uncomfortable, but it's realistic. And really, I think you get the best results when you're just honest. No point faking your way through life. If you're on the fence about going to university, I say, *go for it*. You're not just paying to sit in lectures and do assignments, you're giving yourself the opportunity to discover yourself, gain some direction, and test your determination.
